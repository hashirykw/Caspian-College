Frequently asked questions

Grouped by what people actually ring up about. Where an answer depends on the session rather than on the board's rules, it says so and points you at the office.

01

Choosing a group

Work backwards from the degree rather than forwards from the subject you enjoyed at matric.

  • Write down three degrees you might realistically want in two years. Not one, and not a job title.
  • Look up the entry requirement for each on the university's own admissions page.
  • Find the group that keeps all three open. Usually one does, and it is often not the group the family assumed.
  • Check how each university weighs matric, intermediate and the entry test. That tells you where the next two years of effort should go.

One honest test: if the student cannot sit through a difficult chapter of the identifying subject without losing interest, that group will be a hard two years however good the college is.

Admission counters describe what a group opens. This is the other half.

  • Pre-Medical — without Mathematics, engineering and most computer science degrees are closed afterwards.
  • Pre-Engineering — without Biology, medicine and dentistry are closed. This is the firmer wall of the two.
  • ICS — opens computing; taking Physics as the third elective keeps engineering in reach, taking Statistics narrows it at some universities.
  • General Science — strong for mathematics, statistics, economics and data; weak or closed for medicine and laboratory science degrees.
  • Commerce — opens business, accounting and finance; closes engineering and medicine outright.

Requirements differ between universities and change over time. Verify against the admissions page of the institutions you actually intend to apply to.

Largely, yes. Your matric background sets the boundaries.

  • Matric with Science and Biology generally opens Pre-Medical.
  • Matric with Science and Mathematics generally opens Pre-Engineering, ICS and General Science.
  • Commerce is usually open regardless of matric group.

Within those boundaries the choice is yours, and your marks in the relevant subjects matter more than the aggregate. A student with a high total but weak Mathematics will find Pre-Engineering harder than the total suggests. Colleges also apply their own thresholds on top of the board's rules, so confirm eligibility directly.

There is usually a short window early in Part-I. After board registration is filed, the group is fixed for that session.

  • Whether a seat exists in the group you want.
  • How much teaching you have already missed in the new electives.
  • The board's registration deadline, which is the hard limit.

If the window has closed, completing the current group and changing direction at degree level is usually the better outcome. Repeating Part-I costs a full year, and universities can see it. Ask the college early how long its own window stays open.

02

Admission and paperwork

The standard sequence at private intermediate colleges in Karachi:

  • Call, then visit while classes are running. Bring the student.
  • Submit documents. Ask for originals back the same day.
  • Sit a placement assessment or counselling session, where one applies.
  • Pay against an itemised receipt, with the year's fee schedule in writing.
  • The college files your intermediate registration with the board.

Windows follow the matric result announcement, so dates move every year. Ask for this session's rather than relying on last year's.

Usually required:

  • Matric or SSC result card, original plus photocopies.
  • School leaving certificate.
  • Student B-Form or CNIC, original plus copies.
  • Guardian CNIC copies.
  • Recent passport-size photographs.

From O Level: statement of results plus IBCC equivalence. Joining Part-II from elsewhere: Part-I result, board registration card, and a character certificate with clearance.

Insist on one thing. Originals should be verified and handed straight back. There is no reason for a college to retain them.

O Level grades are not directly comparable to matric, so Pakistani institutions ask for an equivalence certificate issued by the Inter Board Committee of Chairmen. It converts your grades into an equivalent SSC marks total, and both the college and the board need it.

  • Start the application as soon as results are out. Admission windows are short and this is the usual bottleneck.
  • Your O Level subject combination affects which intermediate groups are open to you. Take your statement of results to the college and have it mapped before choosing.
  • Ask whether a provisional seat can be held while equivalence is pending.

Procedures and fees are set by IBCC and change from time to time. Check their current requirements directly.

Yes, and it is fairly common. Three things have to line up: a seat in your group, board rules (registration is already filed, so a group change generally is not possible), and clearance from your previous college.

If your Part-I result was poor, say so plainly at the new college. A place that responds with a concrete plan for the first month is worth more than one that responds with encouragement.

Most colleges will open a provisional file on a school marksheet and complete it when the board announces.

Ask one specific question: does a provisional file hold a seat, or does it merely record your interest? Those are very different things, and groups in high demand fill before the window formally ends.

03

Fees, study and examinations

We do not publish a fee table on this site. A wrong figure costs a family more than no figure, and fees are confirmed by the college in writing at admission.

What we can give you is the list of what to ask, because fee disputes almost never concern the headline number. They concern what was not mentioned in August.

  • Admission charge, and whether it repeats in Part-II.
  • Monthly tuition, and whether it differs by group.
  • What is already inside it: laboratory, journal, library, test and activity charges.
  • Board examination fee, which is set by the board and paid separately.
  • Books, uniform and transport.
  • Concessions, and whether they are reviewed annually or granted once.

The question that settles it: “Please give me the complete schedule of everything I will pay this year, in writing.”

Many students make this transition every year and most manage it. The difficulty is usually vocabulary rather than ability.

  • Build the technical vocabulary of each subject early. The terms recur constantly.
  • Read the English textbook alongside your notes from the first month, not the last.
  • Write answers in English from the start, even badly, rather than translating at examination time.

Ask any college what support exists in the first term. The honest answer varies a great deal, and it is worth hearing before you enrol.

Each science subject carries a practical component, examined separately from the theory paper and conducted with an external examiner. Experiments are recorded in a journal through the year, and the examination involves performing one and answering questions on it.

Students who only watched demonstrations, or copied a file in the final week, tend to struggle in the viva. Ask how often practicals are actually held, whether students perform them individually, and when journals are checked.

Intermediate marks alone rarely decide a competitive admission. Most sought-after programmes add an entry test and then combine the two in their own proportions, which means two universities can rank the same student very differently.

  • Read the aggregate formula on each admissions page rather than assuming a common standard.
  • Your matric result is still contributing two years after you sat it.
  • Negative marking makes examination discipline worth practising deliberately, not just content.

Patterns and weightings are revised, sometimes at short notice. Confirm the current position with the examining body.

The board provides for improvement examinations, but the rules on which papers may be repeated and how the result is reported change from time to time. Confirm the current position with the board or your college.

  • Work out what aggregate your target degrees actually require. Sometimes the improvement is unnecessary.
  • Weigh the cost against Part-II preparation. Carrying both has ended badly for many students.
  • Find out why it happened. A gap in the basics will repeat itself unless it is addressed.

Examinations for colleges in Karachi are conducted by the Board of Intermediate Education Karachi. The board sets the scheme of studies, runs the theory and practical examinations for Part-I and Part-II, and issues the intermediate certificate.

Colleges teach to that scheme rather than setting it. This is why subject combinations look similar across colleges, and why the real differences between them lie in teaching. Ask the college to confirm its affiliation and registration arrangements for your session.
